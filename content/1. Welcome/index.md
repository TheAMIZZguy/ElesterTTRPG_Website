Welcome to the world of Elester! A land where magic runs so deeply in the fabrics of reality and thought that is it synonymous with science and philosophy itself. Fantastical creatures have roamed the world for thousands of years until humanity was birthed as a mild curiosity from the gods, creating the clear divide between great beast and man. Adventures may take one searching across lands of ancient history for lost treasure, hunting beasts deep in the wastelands for precious potion ingredients, or navigating the complex intricacies of inter-species political plots and wars.

## Who is this meant for?
Elester TTRPG is a fantasy Tabletop Roleplaying Game (TTRPG) based on ideas from Dungeons & Dragons (D&D) and Pathfinder. It is an interactive story told in part by a [[Terminology#^e897f2|Game Master (GM)]], who deals with rulings and dictating events, and players, who control the characters in the game and traverse the world. The world contains magic, and maintains a technological area of the late-medieval to early renaissance periods, where swords and bows are still dominant in the world. Conflict often arises from battling beasts and antagonistic enemies, but can also range from traps in a dungeon, or from a rival who is framing the party for murder, among countless possibilities.  

The game is meant to be played with a party of four players, but with proper balancing and mutual respect for time, it can range to fit differences. Due to the large number of rules one may be subject to at the start, it is recommended that players have some previous experience with a previous TTRPG (or Baldur's Gate!), and for the GM to be experienced with a TTRPG similar to Pathfinder or D&D. But the rules themselves are straightforward and this rulebook will try to be as accessible for players of any level of experience, so if you are willing to learn the rules as you play, then you should not be worried about needing any more experience other than knowing what the fantasy genre is!

## Flow of the Game
ElesterTTRPG is played in a series of sessions, often around four hours a piece, which work much like the chapters of a book. From the first character that is introduced, to the conclusion of the story (often called a [[Terminology#^e897f2|campaign]]), games can be as short as a single session (a [[Terminology#^e897f2|oneshot]]), or span years. Like with many things that will be discussed in this ruleset, it is strongly recommended that everyone starts a campaign with a clear idea of expectations to how long sessions, meetings, and campaigns should last. 

A common element of a session is battles, with many of the aspects of a character and most of the pages of this ruleset detailing how such encounters would work. The recommended amount often equates to around one to two battles per session, but this depends on the group itself, and a party that prefers the core aspect of roleplaying may never come across an encounter as they sneak around foes or solve mysteries.

## The Players
There are two types of players in ElesterTTRPG, the first is the Game Master, and the second is everyone else. For simplicity, *player* will refer to anyone in the game that is not the GM and the collective of players playing in the same group is the *party*, the term *table* may be used on occasion to refer to the players and the GM together. Players control characters, intuitively called Playable Characters (PCs). Most people playing a TTRPG for the first time will be players as they familiarize themselves with the rules and and learn their style of playing. And the recommended number of players is usually 4.

At the start of a campaign, or before a group meets up to play, players will often create these characters alongside their personality, backstories, statistics, abilities, and all other things to make the characters feel real. Collaborating with other players and the GM can make for interesting character dynamics and greater motivation to interact with the world. While this ruleset has very defined limitations on character creation, the only real limit is the GM's guidelines and the players' imaginations.

To play the game, players describe the actions they want to characters to take and may need to roll dice and use their abilities to succeed. Wherein the GM then describes the result of the actions taken. Some players prefer to really act out the experience (roleplaying) and be their character, and others enjoy the feeling of dictating what their characters would do, as if narrating a story. Both of these styles can exist in unison, but it is important as a player to make sure everyone can participate in the game in a way that is enjoyable to them. 


## The Game Master
While players control characters, the GM controls the world. The GM descries the situations the PCs experience in a campaign, act out the other people in the world --Non-Playable Characters (NPCs)-- and interprets the story. GMs also have several different styles, some prefer descriptive scenery and run stories going into the detailed history to the world, while another may emphasize a story where the party gets to feel like great heroes and primarily follow the rule of cool. Being very clear on what type of campaign the table wants can be a great way to make sure the game starts smoothly. Topics that the table should discuss with the GM include what type of experiences the party wants, the style that the GM prefers, any lay out any rules the table may want to change/add, including any potential topics that may be best avoided. 

Being a GM is a challenge, at the basics requiring you to be comfortable with the rules so that gameplay may run smoothly, and to help out newer players. But also be able to narrate the story while juggling potentially dozens of NPC motivations and immediate session planning. The payoff to this is the satisfaction of telling a great story with your players, and the freedom of setting that can be difficult to achieve anywhere else.   


## What you need to play
1) A copy of the ruleset 
2) Character sheets; and notes on your character
3) A set of polyhedral dice (more than the simple six sided die)
4) Setting/Adventure; either designed by the GM or using an existing one
5) Maps and Miniatures, to visualize settings of combat and the represent key areas

All of these items are optional and can be swapped out if one has a plan on how they may replaced. Common replacements are to use theatre-of-mind (imagination) in lieu of maps or online rollers for dice.
